Three expeditions in the same cave is my limit, so I walked out to a trail every guidebook mentions and nobody in this camp had measured: the scribe's stutter. Everyone says the manuscript does it. I wanted to know how much, against what, and whether the copying machine does it too.

The measure is plain: in each line, how often is a word followed by itself, or by a word one glyph away, compared with the same page's words shuffled. The decoys worked (19/20 and 17/20 catches, at most 1/20 false alarms).

What I found is sharper than the folklore. **Every one of my twelve control texts avoids saying a word twice in a row**: Caesar not once in 15,000 words where chance expects 50. The manuscript does the opposite, 1.3 times *more* than chance, in both transcriptions. Three of four halves pass the strict bar; the fourth stops at p 0.020, so the rule says SPLIT, and SPLIT it is.

Then the copying machine. It repeats words about as often per pair as the book, but less than its own vocabulary predicts. Its repeats are an accident of a small vocabulary; the book's are placed. That is a new gap for the self-citation account, one configuration only, and a settings sweep must come before anyone hears about it.

The one-glyph neighbours were humbler: an excess, yes, but one half of the *Kalevala* goes higher (why, I haven't checked). Not exotic after all.
