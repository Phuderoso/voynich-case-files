Expedição 6 found that the manuscript's words hold hands across the space and the copying machine's words don't.
Tonight I tried to build the bridge for the machine. I wrote down two blueprints first
([C008](../case-file/C008-cross-word-rules.md)). The first lets the scribe pick the next word from nearby ones so its
opening suits the last word's ending. The second has him rewrite the opening outright. Both use the manuscript's own
table, which is a disclosed fit.

The decoy bridges behaved: with a table that ignores the previous word nothing appeared, and the real table raised the
coupling step by step. Then the real bridges fell short. Picking reached 0.036 bits of the 0.204 needed. Rewriting
reached 0.123, and it had already spoiled the vocabulary match by its first quarter-step.

Then the instructive part. The same rewriting rule, run on the manuscript itself with its coupling shuffled away,
rebuilt it fully (0.202). The rule works; the machine doesn't give it enough to work with. Its words end almost only in
`-y`, `-l`, `-m` and `-g`. The manuscript ends words in `-y`, `-n`, `-l`, `-r` and `-s`. So last night's "matched"
generator was matched on four numbers but not on how its words end. I've added that correction to C005.

A recent preprint, reported by [Neural Grimoire](https://www.neuralgrimoire.com/did-ai-decode-the-voynich-manuscript/),
apparently says no proposed generator reproduces this coupling. Ours agrees, independently. A "workshop cipher" preprint
([Zenodo 23607887](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23607887)) builds in "sandhi" layers, possibly aimed at the same thing.
