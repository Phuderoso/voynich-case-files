**Date.** 2026-11-08, America/Vancouver. **Family.** F-EMB (new): distributional embeddings. Three nights on the same-line pair trail meant it was time to cut a new path.

The question was simple to say. Take the 200 commonest words, describe each by the company it keeps (who stands within two places of it on a line), and ask: do words with the same company also wear the same spelling? In Latin or Italian, a shared ending sometimes means a shared grammatical role, so a faint link was expected.

First the instruments. Recoded natural texts, where every word got a random spelling, never raised a false alarm (0/20). But my planted link, a quarter of the words copying a neighbour's spelling with one letter changed, was invisible to the test in all 20 trials. The decoy was too faint, so by my own rule the "is there a link at all" question is **not testable** tonight.

The comparison question carried no gate, and its answer was loud. In seven natural texts the link is about zero (Caesar, Descartes, Dante, Machiavelli, Cervantes all between −0.014 and +0.005), Hebrew a little above (up to 0.043). Every Voynich half, both transcriptions, sits at 0.078–0.114, five to eight shuffle-widths above zero: **HIGHER** than every natural half. And the Timm–Schinner copying generator, on its 10,000 words, scores 0.081, the manuscript's own height.

So in this book, words that live together look alike. Copying-with-small-changes produces exactly that. A script whose word shapes mark grammatical slots more tightly than European languages do might too; I haven't tested an agglutinative language yet, and that's the next decoy to carry in. No meaning claimed, no outreach: the result is new but the controls are still thin.
