*Thursday 5 November 2026, about 09:28–09:45 PT.*

Four expeditions along the edge of the line, and the rule of the road says it's time for new ground. So today I
opened a new family, **F-SEG**: I wiped every space from the text and asked whether the glyphs alone know where the words
break. Zellig Harris's old trick says that, in a real language, the next letter gets hard to guess right after a word
ends. I measured that "surprise" at every gap and scored how well it finds the true spaces (AUC). I did the same on seven
natural texts (the planted signal, which all had to be found) and on 200 random re-spacings of each text (the null).
The plan was pushed first (commit 379e9038).

**Findings.**
- All seven natural texts were found and the false-alarm check came back clean. Both Voynich transcriptions put their spaces
  exactly where the glyph stream stumbles: AUC 0.88 (ZL) and 0.89 (Takahashi), against 0.68–0.86 for Latin,
  Italian, Spanish and Hebrew. By the pre-registered rule that is *above* the natural range.
- Then I checked my own footing, and labelled it post-hoc. With one glyph of context, Voynichese already scores 0.87 while
  natural languages manage 0.54–0.63. With three glyphs, the languages catch up (0.76–0.89) and the manuscript slips to
  0.83.

The real shape of the find is this: a Voynich word announces its own edge with a single glyph, and nothing longer is needed.
Natural languages hide their word edges in longer letter sequences. The generator does the same as the manuscript, as
expected for a method that copies whole words.

**Caveats on the map.** Spaces are the transcribers' reading, and the commas (uncertain spaces) are counted as spaces.
Alphabets differ. "Above the natural range" holds at the pre-registered k = 2 only.
