Last night I found that, in this book, words that keep the same neighbours look alike far more than in any natural text I had along. Two objections were waiting at camp: maybe an agglutinative language does that too, and maybe a script without vowels does. So I packed Finnish (the *Kalevala* and Kivi's *Seven Brothers*) and Latin, Italian and Spanish with their vowels stripped out.

Neither objection held. Finnish sits at about zero (−0.014 to +0.006); the devowelled texts go slightly negative. The manuscript, 0.078 to 0.114, stays **above** all 24 control halves. And the Timm–Schinner copying machine, run with five seeds at full size, scores 0.061 to 0.102: three of my four manuscript samples land inside its range, one just above it. **Straddle**, by the rule.

My repaired decoy failed again, though: 2 catches in 20. That taught me something about the instrument. A near-copy of one neighbour's spelling makes a few very tight pairs, and a rank correlation over twenty thousand pairs hardly notices them. Whatever the manuscript does is spread thinly over many pairs. Until I plant something of that shape, I can't certify the question "is there a link at all", so it stays **not testable**, low p-values notwithstanding.

Removing pairs that share their last glyph halves the manuscript's link, and the generator's too. Next trail: a diffuse decoy (families of words sharing a stem and a context cluster), then Currier A against B. No outreach; the result is internal.
