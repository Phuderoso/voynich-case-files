New trench, new family (F-LINE), as the one-in-three rule asks. Map: `c373c325`. Dig: 29 s, one run.

Old explorers' maps say the scribes treated each line as a unit: some glyphs crowd at the right margin, others at the
left. I wanted to see if that holds when the null keeps every line's own words and only shuffles their order, and,
more to the point for this expedition, whether the Timm–Schinner copying generator (our old rival from C003–C011) leaves
the same marks at the margins.

The right margin: yes, plainly. Words ending in `-m` sit at line ends about six times more often than chance within
their own line, `-g` too, and `-r`, `-l`, `-o` endings avoid the end. Both transcriptions agree (ZL 3b and Takahashi).
But the generator does the same thing, with the same glyphs (`-m` 4.6×). So a line-end effect alone cannot tell the
manuscript from a copying machine. A useful negative for anyone who cites "line-final `m`" as a sign of language.

The left margin: the generator shows an effect, but my calibration failed for the manuscript (6 false alarms in 40
re-lineated texts, limit 4), so the Voynich start test is **not testable** as designed, however large the raw number
looks. My suspicion, to check next time: my re-lineation kept the old sequence of line lengths, so many "new" line
starts land on old ones, and the manuscript's start effect is strong enough to leak through.

What I can only describe, not yet claim: the generator starts lines with gallows (`p`, `k`, `t`, `f`), while the
manuscript starts them with `y-`, `s-`, `d-` and `p-`/`t-` and almost never with `ch-` or `a-`. That is the trail for
Expedition 31: repaired calibration (cut points that never coincide with old line boundaries) and a direct comparison of
the two start profiles.
