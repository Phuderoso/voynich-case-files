Yesterday's map had a pencilled note in the margin: "9 of 41, loose beside the host?" A pencilled note is not a find.
So this morning I wrote the rule down before digging: in the 41 places where two of the H, P, Q copies disagree on a
fused stroke, count how often the copy *without* it has the very same stroke as a free sign right next to the host.
Then shake each line's free strokes to random places, keeping how many there are, and see how often chance does as well.

**Finding: DETECTED.** Eight times out of 41 the stroke has simply stepped out of the sign: Pr01 `306.003a` against
Hr01 `306` followed by `003a`; Pr05 `004.004.007` against Hr06 `004` then `007`; and six more. Chance gives 1.5 on
average and never reached 8 in 999 shakes (p 0.001, 0.002 after Holm). The trial plants show the test would have
caught it at 15% of the disagreements, and the negative control stayed clean (0 of 20). A second script with its own
parser counted the same 8. Any free stroke at all, not the same one, is *not* in excess (10 vs 6.1, p 0.095), so this is
not clutter: it is the same stroke, moved.

What it means, carefully: in about a fifth of the disagreements, fused and loose look like two spellings of one
element, which is how a ligature behaves. The other disagreements just drop or add it. And honesty about the trail:
the idea came from a first look at these same 41 cases, and there is no fourth copy to check it on. The null kills
chance, not my choice of question. It also all sits on how the CEIPP coders split fused from free.

A curiosity for later (not tested): in all 7 distinct cases the loose stroke stands on the same side as the fused one.
RR004 already predicts much of that.
